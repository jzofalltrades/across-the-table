    How to play

    1. Lift a piece. You play white. Click one of your pieces and its legal squares glow green.
    2. Make your move. Click a green square. Any other square flashes red, just like the real board.
    3. Press the space bar. Use your keyboard, the amber Send move button, or the bar on the board itself.
    4. Watch the arm reply. It reaches across, picks up the opponent's piece and plays it. Amber squares show its move. A red king means check.
    5. Undo with the red button. Before you send, it cancels your move. After the reply, it takes back the last pair: the arm reverses its own move and a blue square shows where your piece goes back.
    6. Train with the coach. Stockfish rates every move you send and can show a hint. Finished games are kept in the board's memory so you can see yourself improve.
    7. Compare the arms. Switch between the articulated and SCARA designs at the top, and press Show electronics to see inside.

      How the gripper holds a piece

      1. Come down open. The arm lowers the gripper with the jaws apart, wide enough to pass the head, collar and crown.
      2. One servo closes both jaws. The grip servo turns a pinion. Two racks on a linear rail slide the fingers in by exactly the same amount, so they always meet centred.
      3. Hooks wrap the neck. The flat curved hooks at the bottom of each finger close around the neck just under the collar. On a rook they go under the turret lip.
      4. The collar sits on the hooks. On the lift the piece hangs from its collar, so even a loose grip can't drop it. The TPU lining stops it twisting.
      5. Six heights, one motion. The Pi knows which piece is on each square, so it lowers to that piece's grip height: 35 mm for a pawn up to 52 mm for a king.

      Rules for the chess set

      Neck under 14 mm wide so the hooks can close. Collar or lip over 18 mm so the piece can't slip through. Jaws open at least 10 mm per side to pass the widest part above the grip.

      Inside every piece

      Each piece is cut in half here. Its base holds a steel washer, a disc magnet and a felt pad. White pieces have the magnet's north pole facing down, black pieces the south pole. The amber marks show where the hook jaws grip.

      How the board reads it

      One analog hall sensor sits under every square. The pole facing down sets which way the field passes through it, so a single sensor tells the Pi three things: white piece, empty or black piece.

      The insert

      • N52 disc magnetAbout 12 × 3 mm, glued into a pocket in the base. The drawings exaggerate it so the poles show.
      • Steel washerAdds weight so pieces feel solid, and backs the magnet so more of its field points down at the sensor.
      • Felt padCovers the magnet, protects the board and keeps every piece the same distance from its sensor.

      Polarity rule

      • White: north pole downThe sensor's output drops to about 1 V.
      • Black: south pole downThe output rises to about 4 V.
      • Empty squareThe output sits at about 2.5 V, half the supply.

      Why it matters

      Knowing the colour on each square lets the Pi pin down every move, including captures that occupancy alone can't tell apart: when your knight takes on e5, e5 flips from black to white.

      Build checks

      • Mark poles before gluingUse a compass or a marked reference magnet. One flipped magnet makes that piece read as the wrong colour.
      • Test the gap firstThe magnet sits about 8–10 mm above the sensor. The sensor-strip prototype checks the reading is strong there and a piece on the next square stays too weak to count.
      • Calibrate onceThe Pi records each square's empty, white and black voltages, since sensors and magnets vary slightly.

      Why an articulated arm

      Commit, cancel, undo

      The hall sensors can't tell when you've finished a move, so nothing is sent until you press the space bar. The red button cancels before sending, or takes back a full move after. Online, that takeback goes to the opponent as a request they accept, which the Lichess Board API supports.

      A board that remembers

      The Pi keeps every game, move by move, with Stockfish's verdict on each of your moves. Over time it shows whether your blunders are falling and which phase of the game costs you most, which turns the board into a training partner.

      Captures go first

      When the opponent captures, the arm clears your piece to the graveyard tray before making its own move. Castling moves king then rook. Promotion swaps in a queen.

      One grip, six heights

      The hook jaws close under each piece's collar (under the lip for rooks). The arm lowers to a height looked up from the piece type, from 35 mm for a pawn to 52 mm for a king.

      Inside the platform

      Under each square sits a WS2812 LED and an analog hall sensor on eight strip boards. Four 16-channel muxes feed the Pi, which runs the game and Stockfish and talks to the arm's ESP32 over UART. A 24 V supply feeds the stepper drivers, with a 5 V buck for the Pi and LEDs.
